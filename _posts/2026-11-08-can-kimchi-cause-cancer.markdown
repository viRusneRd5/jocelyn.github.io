_The science behind the claims that fermented foods increase the risk of stomach cancer_

{% include figure image_path="/assets/images/kimchi.jpg" alt="Bowl of kimchi" caption="Kimchi, a traditional fermented food. Photo from [Unsplash](https://unsplash.com/)." %}

While doomscrolling on Instagram, I came across a [reel](https://www.instagram.com/reel/DeRrJoChh-7/?xtok=OXFjMnp4bnYzYmMz) by [@drjeffyoo](https://www.instagram.com/drjeffyoo?srtk=MW94OHZuNW45djl1Ng==), an emergency physician and health commentator, interviewing gastroenterologist Dr Eric M. Yoshida about gut health. Dr Yoshida recommends avoiding alcohol and fermented foods, and claims that Japan and Korea have the highest rates of stomach cancer, followed by China. He points to studies in China showing high rates of stomach cancer among people who had less access to fresh fruit and vegetables and relied on fermented produce instead.

The reel clearly struck a nerve. In a [follow-up video](https://www.instagram.com/reel/Det_6FUB2lD/?dlrf=M2k3ZHF2N3htdnpq), Dr Yoo said “a lot of people are now afraid to eat fermented food”, and were asking whether kimchi, kombucha or yoghurt meant they would get cancer. Dr Yoshida replied “it's in moderation”, suggested there is likely a large genetic component to the higher risk in East Asian countries, and recommended changing environmental risk factors such as alcohol, smoking and fermented foods.

**The short answer:** some fermented foods are linked to a higher risk of stomach cancer, but salt, _H. pylori_ and how much fresh produce you eat seem to matter more.

## What does the science say?

**Systematic review:** gathers all the published studies on a question, using set rules for which ones to include, and summarises what they found. \
**Meta-analysis:** goes further and combines those results statistically, as if they were one much larger study. Because they draw on thousands of people, both are stronger evidence than any single study. \
**Statistically significant:** the result is unlikely to be due to chance alone. It doesn’t mean the effect is large or important, just that it probably isn’t a fluke.
{: .notice}

A [2022 study](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.eclinm.2022.101404) confirmed that East Asian countries have the highest rates of stomach cancer, with men in Japan, Mongolia and Korea having the highest rates in the world, and men overall about twice as likely as women to be diagnosed. But this study estimated new cases and deaths from population data alone. It had no information on what anyone actually ate, and instead made assumptions about dietary habits based on geography.

It did highlight one important risk factor: _Helicobacter pylori_, a very common bacteria that can infect the stomach. It spreads from person to person, often through poor hygiene, but also through contaminated food or water. _H. pylori_ can cause gastritis (inflammation of the stomach lining), which can lead to ulcers, and long-term gastritis can lead to stomach cancer. It can be treated with antibiotics, and vaccines are under development.

**Did you know?** [Two Australian physician-scientists](https://www.nobelprize.org/prizes/medicine/2005/7693-the-nobel-prize-in-physiology-or-medicine-2005-2005-6/), Barry Marshall and Robin Warren, were awarded the Nobel Prize for discovering the link between this bacteria and stomach ulcers.
{: .notice--info}

Two meta-analyses have put a number on the pickled vegetable risk, and both landed in the same place: people who ate the most pickled vegetables were about 1.28 times as likely to develop stomach cancer ([2020, 232 studies](https://doi.org/10.4178/epih.e2020004); [2021, 26 studies](https://doi.org/10.3389/fnut.2021.801228)). Moderate intake had no significant effect.

Salt looks like the bigger culprit. The 2021 analysis found high and moderate salt intake was associated with 1.25 and 1.2 times the risk, and the 2020 review suggested a range of 1.3 to 3.8 times, although it didn’t have enough studies to pool the results properly. This is relevant to kimchi, which is often high in salt, and it suggests some of the risk attached to pickled and fermented vegetables comes from their salt content rather than from fermentation itself. A high-salt diet may [damage the gastric mucosal barrier and cause chronic inflammation](https://doi.org/10.1002/eji.70234), and [exacerbate _H. pylori_ infection](https://doi.org/10.3390/jpm13091325).

Fresh produce pulls in the other direction. In the same 2020 review, people who ate fruit or vegetables more than three times a week were around half as likely to develop stomach cancer (0.5 times for fruit and 0.6 times for vegetables).

Fermented meats come with a separate warning. In 2018, the World Health Organization's International Agency for Research on Cancer (IARC) [reported](https://publications.iarc.who.int/Book-And-Report-Series/Iarc-Monographs-On-The-Identification-Of-Carcinogenic-Hazards-To-Humans/Red-Meat-And-Processed-Meat-2018) that processed meat, which includes fermented meats, is “carcinogenic to humans”: studies of people provided enough evidence that it causes bowel cancer. Red meat was classified as “probably carcinogenic”. Salting, curing, fermenting and smoking can all create cancer-causing chemicals, including _N_-nitroso compounds. Polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons are a concern too, but they come from smoking and high-heat cooking rather than fermentation, so they only apply to fermented meats that are also smoked.

Those _N_-nitroso compounds are worth understanding, because they turn up again in a [2009 meta-analysis](https://doi.org/10.1038/sj.bjc.6605372) that found a statistically significant link between pickled vegetables and oesophageal cancer. The review reported fungal contamination of the pickled vegetables, which may increase the amount of nitrates.

Nitrates on their own aren’t a major concern, and most of the nitrates we eat come from vegetables. The problem is that bacteria and fungi, including those that contaminate food or take part in fermentation, can convert nitrates into nitrites. In the acidic environment of the stomach or of a fermented food, nitrites react with compounds from the protein in our food to form _N_-nitroso compounds. Many of these cause cancer in [animal studies](https://doi.org/10.1002/ijc.2910270408) and have been linked to [stomach and bowel cancer in people](https://doi.org/10.3945/ajcn.111.012377). Vitamin C, found in fresh fruit and vegetables, can [block their formation](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jtbi.2026.112444) — which may be one reason fresh produce is linked to a lower risk.

It isn’t clear exactly which study Dr Yoshida was referring to, but I found two Chinese studies that looked at fruit and vegetable intake and cancer risk. Both found that eating more was linked to a lower risk, and neither looked at fermented foods. [The first](https://doi.org/10.1002/ijc.70697) found that people who ate more vegetables were also less likely to be overweight, smoke or have a chronic disease, and were generally more physically active, so these other habits may explain part of the lower risk. [The second](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11670-011-0171-7) didn’t take into account which types of fruit and vegetables people ate, or how they were prepared. Separately, [a third Chinese study](https://doi.org/10.3389/fnut.2022.997773) linked a high-salt diet to more illness and early death overall, not just cancer.

As for the genetic component Dr Yoshida raised, only 1% to 3% of stomach cancers [are inherited](https://www.cancer.gov/pediatric-adult-rare-tumor/rare-tumors/rare-digestive-system-tumors/hereditary-diffuse-gastric-cancer).

## Summary

There is some evidence linking fermented foods with an increased risk of stomach cancer, but salt and _H. pylori_ look more important than fermentation itself, and regularly eating fresh fruit and vegetables is linked with a decreased risk. It’s also worth remembering that these studies only look at the association between what people ate and whether they developed cancer. Many other factors can be involved, and correlation (two things happening together) doesn’t necessarily mean causation (one causing the other).

So, will I be avoiding fermented foods? If I were to, where would I stop? Do I avoid fermented vegetables and legumes, but keep eating yoghurt, cheese and chocolate? In my (unqualified to give dietary advice) opinion, the evidence isn’t strong enough to cut them out entirely, as long as my diet is balanced with the recommended daily intake of fruit and vegetables — the Australian Government’s [guide to healthy eating](https://www.eatforhealth.gov.au/sites/default/files/2022-10/n55_agthe_large.pdf) is a good place to start. I’m a strong believer in everything in moderation, but hopefully I’ve given you enough information to make your own informed decision.

Fermented foods also come with real benefits, and a few genuinely strange ones. That’s the subject of my next post.
