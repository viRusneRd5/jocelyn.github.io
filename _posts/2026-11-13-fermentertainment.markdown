_The science behind fermented foods_

{% include figure image_path="/assets/images/pickled-vegetables.jpg" alt="Jars of pickled vegetables" caption="Pickled vegetables. Photo from [Unsplash](https://unsplash.com/)." %}

While researching my previous article, [_Can kimchi cause cancer?_]({% post_url 2026-11-08-can-kimchi-cause-cancer %}), I learned so many fun facts about fermentation that the background section kept growing. So I split it into two posts. This one covers some of the fermented foods I discovered, their fun facts, and the science on other health concerns and benefits.

## What is fermentation, and why do we do it?

We often think of fermentation as turning sugars into alcohol, which humans have done since ancient times. But fermentation is also behind bread and yoghurt, and is used to preserve many foods. If you're here for the cancer question, [see my previous article]({% post_url 2026-11-08-can-kimchi-cause-cancer %}).

[There are four main types of fermentation](https://doi.org/10.1016/B978-0-12-816685-7.00003-3):

1. **Alcohol fermentation:** yeasts break down sugars into ethanol.
2. **Lactic acid fermentation:** lactic acid bacteria turn sugars into lactic acid. The acid stops other (possibly harmful) bacteria from growing and keeps the food from spoiling.
3. **Acetic acid fermentation:** acetic acid bacteria convert sugars or ethanol into acetic acid.
4. **Alkaline fermentation:** _Bacillus_ bacteria break proteins down into amino acids and ammonia, which raises the food's pH.

Fermentation can also add flavour and aroma to bland or bitter foods, enrich them with essential amino acids, vitamins and protein, remove compounds that stop our bodies absorbing nutrients, and make cooking faster and cheaper.

## A tour of fermented foods

**Yoghurt and cheese:** [yoghurt starts out as milk.](https://agriculture.institute/dairy-products-iii/understanding-yoghurt-production-techniques/) Two types of lactic acid bacteria convert the lactose (milk sugar) into lactic acid, turning the milk into yoghurt. Keeping it cold slows the bacteria, which stops it becoming too acidic. Cheese starts the same way, then rennet separates the milk solids from the liquid whey, and [these solids become cheese](https://theconversation.com/cheesemaking-is-a-complex-science-a-food-chemist-explains-the-process-from-milk-to-mozzarella-230829). [Rennet can come from animals](https://doi.org/10.1016/B978-0-323-89929-1.00036-6), but is now commonly made by fermenting a fungus called _Rhizomucor miehei_, or by genetically modified microorganisms that produce the same enzymes. Until now, I never appreciated that my scientific brain may have come from my mother, a home scientist who made yoghurt and cheese throughout my childhood.

**Olives:** [raw olives are intensely bitter](https://www.delallo.com/blog/all-about-the-best-olives/) and must be cured and fermented to remove oleuropein and other bitter compounds. Most methods use a salt brine and lactic acid bacteria.

**Vinegar:** [vinegar goes through two rounds of fermentation.](https://www.discoveryuk.com/how-its-made/how-is-vinegar-made/) First, yeast converts sugar into ethanol, then acetic acid bacteria convert the ethanol into acetic acid. The starting ingredient, ageing time and method give each vinegar its distinct flavour.

**Chocolate:** cocoa beans ferment naturally in banana leaves, baskets or wooden boxes, with the help of yeasts, lactic acid bacteria and acetic acid bacteria. This develops their flavour and breaks down the pulp around them. Because it relies on the surrounding environment, the flavour of chocolate can [depend on where the beans were fermented](https://www.abc.net.au/news/2026-08-14/australian-cocoa-and-chocolate-flavour-comes-from-fermentation/107029174). The beans are then [cleaned, dried and roasted](https://www.whitakerschocolates.com/blogs/blog/how-is-chocolate-made-step-by-step) before being made into chocolate.

**Kimchi:** when travelling in South Korea, I learned that kimchi was a way to preserve abundant summer vegetables for winter, when fresh produce was scarce. Traditionally, cabbages and radishes were packed into ceramic pots and buried in the ground, usually without the garlic and chilli in modern kimchi. [The cabbage is soaked in a salt](https://mykoreankitchen.com/kimchi-recipe/) brine before fermentation, so kimchi can be [high in salt](https://www.harvardhealth.org/diet-and-nutrition/fermented-foods-should-they-be-part-of-your-diet).

**Did you know?** Kimchi [fed South Korean troops during the Vietnam War](https://www.vice.com/en/article/how-kimchi-was-used-as-political-leverage-in-the-vietnam-war/), and the first Korean in space took a specially developed [space kimchi to the International Space Station](https://www.npr.org/2008/02/24/19320175/enjoying-kimchi-in-space). I wonder which Australian food will go to space with the [first astronaut to fly under the Australian flag](https://www.abc.net.au/news/2026-10-14/sa-australian-of-the-year-bennell-pegg-astronaut-decision/107263722). Perhaps Vegemite… speaking of fermentation…

**Vegemite:** this Australian spread isn't fermented itself, but it is made from the brewer's yeast extract left over from beer fermentation, much like Marmite in the UK.

**Did you know?** Vegemite was the [first product electronically scanned at an Australian checkout](https://vegemite.com.au/100-mitey-years/), and it [fed Australian troops during World War II](https://vegemite.com.au/heritage/the-vegemite-story/) because of its nutritional value.
{: .notice--info}

**Natto:** after proofreading this article, my friend Donna told me about natto, a traditional Japanese food and a classic example of alkaline fermentation. Boiled soybeans are mixed with _Bacillus_ bacteria and incubated for 24 to 48 hours, which coats them in a sticky slime. The beans are then stirred vigorously with chopsticks until they bind in sticky strings. Natto is often served with rice, sushi or toast, or stirred through miso soup. Like Vegemite for Australians, it’s a love-it-or-hate-it food.

**Pazhamkanji:** when I told my colleague and friend Kevin about my findings, he told me about pazhamkanji, a traditional dish from his home state of Kerala, in southern India. Leftover rice is soaked in water and fermented overnight in a clay pot, then often served with curd, chillies or other vegetables. It is a staple breakfast for elderly people, and is said to [give energy and prolong life](https://mygrandmas-healthyschool.com/2018/05/22/blog-1-fermented-rice-pazhamkanji/).

**Pickled vegetables and sauerkraut:** [cucumbers were probably first fermented in the Middle East around 2000 BC](https://doi.org/10.1016/B978-0-12-384947-2.00545-6), and labourers building the Great Wall of China were likely eating fermented vegetables around 300 BC. However, not all pickles are fermented. ["Quick pickling"](https://spicestoryteller.com/the-science-behind-pickling-why-it-works/) means soaking food in vinegar for a few hours or days; the acidity stops spoilage bacteria from growing, which preserves the vegetables. Fermented pickles use a salt brine instead, which under the right conditions stops spoilage bacteria while encouraging lactic acid bacteria. You may have caught me out on a technicality: are quick pickles technically fermented if the vinegar used to make them was itself fermented?

**Did you know?** Although the name sauerkraut comes from the German for “sour cabbage”, the [earliest mention comes from China 2,000 years ago](https://doi.org/10.1016/B978-0-12-802309-9.00024-8), and it wasn't introduced to Europe until 1,000 years later. Romans ate it to prevent intestinal infections, and sailors ate it to prevent scurvy.
{: .notice--info}

**Fermented meats:** [some sausages are preserved](https://doi.org/10.1002/9781118522653.ch2) by drying the meat with salt or nitrates, or by smoking it, while lactic acid bacteria make the meat more acidic. [Nitrates are often](https://agriculture.institute/processed-meat-technology/fermented-meat-products-guide/) added to prevent the growth of bacteria that produce botulism toxin.

This list is far from complete! Many other foods are fermented, including miso paste, stinky tofu, tea, Worcestershire sauce, sour cream, ginger beer, soy sauce, bread and shrimp paste. Wikipedia has a [huge list of fermented foods](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_fermented_foods) if you'd like more. Fermentation isn't just for food, either. [The pharmaceutical industry](https://ritaibioreactor.com/pharmaceutical-fermentation-process/) uses it to produce antibiotics, vaccines and other medicines, and it's also used to [make biofuels](https://www.monash.edu/student-academic-success/biology/biotechnological-applications-of-biochemical-pathways/anaerobic-fermentation-of-biomass-for-fuel-production) and [treat wastewater](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jenvman.2024.121724)!

## Other health concerns

If it isn't done correctly, fermentation can encourage the growth of bacteria that produce [botulism toxin](https://www.cdc.gov/botulism/about/index.html). Botulism, the disease it causes, is rare but serious because the toxin attacks the nerves. [Food Standards Australia New Zealand (FSANZ)](https://www.foodstandards.gov.au/sites/default/files/2025-08/Compendium%20of%20Microbiological%20Criteria%202025.pdf) gives regulators and the food industry guidance on harmful microorganisms, how they occur, and how to prevent their growth.

Traditional fermentation has countless methods worldwide, but it must be done under strict conditions, especially in commercial production. For example, [many fermented foods must have a pH below 4.6](https://www.ift.org/food-technology-magazine/the-basics-about-acid-levels-in-food) (be acidic enough) to prevent botulism toxin-producing bacteria from growing. Foods above this pH must be refrigerated and have a short shelf life.

People with histamine sensitivity or intolerance are [advised to avoid fermented foods](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/pmid/28223894/), as these foods are naturally high in histamine.

## Are there any health benefits to eating fermented foods?

Many fermented foods contain probiotics: live bacteria that help keep a healthy balance of bacteria in your gut, which is important for [digestion, nutrient absorption, immune function and metabolism](https://www.harvardhealth.org/diet-and-nutrition/fermented-foods-should-they-be-part-of-your-diet). Studies have also linked fermented foods to a lower risk of some cancers and type 2 diabetes, and to help with managing food allergies. Let's look at some of the evidence.

A [systematic review](https://doi.org/10.1093/nutrit/nuaa013) found a link between dairy consumption (fermented or not) and increased risk of prostate cancer, but reported that fermented milk was associated with a lower risk of breast cancer, colorectal cancer and type 2 diabetes, along with other benefits. A [meta-analysis](https://doi.org/10.3389/fonc.2022.812679) found that eating cheese or yoghurt was associated with a lower risk of colorectal (bowel) cancer, and a [systematic review and meta-analysis of 61 studies](https://doi.org/10.1002/ijc.31959) reported a lower risk of several cancers among people who ate fermented dairy foods. A [review of 10 studies](https://doi.org/10.3389/fnut.2025.1689636) reported promising effects of fermented foods in managing food allergies.

Beyond boosting probiotic activity (introducing healthy bacteria that compete with unhealthy bacteria in your gut), [fermented milk and other fermented foods](https://doi.org/10.3390/nu11051189) can increase antioxidant activity, help lower blood pressure and increase vitamin content. [Another review](https://doi.org/10.3389/fnut.2025.1677478) highlights the role of traditional fermented foods in sustainable food systems and the importance of preserving these practices.

A [2026 study](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.biortech.2026.135905) split 16 mice into a control group and a treatment group. All the mice were exposed to nanoplastics (plastic particles even smaller than microplastics), but the treatment group was first fed the lactic acid bacteria involved in kimchi fermentation. The treatment group had significantly more nanoplastics in their faeces than the control group, suggesting that fewer nanoplastics built up in their guts.

This is a single study in a small number of mice, so it shouldn't carry the same weight as large systematic reviews and meta-analyses, and more research is needed. However, the results are promising and point to other possible benefits of fermented foods.

## The bottom line

Fermentation is one of humanity's oldest food technologies, and it's hiding in far more of your pantry than you might expect, from bread and chocolate to Vegemite. The research so far is promising, with fermented foods (especially dairy) linked to lower risks of several conditions, although these are associations rather than proof of cause and effect. Made properly, fermented foods are a tasty, varied addition to most diets, with histamine intolerance the main exception to keep in mind.
